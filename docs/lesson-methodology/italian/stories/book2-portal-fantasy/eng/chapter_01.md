# Chapter 1 — First Steps

---

Misha stood before the door in his room, his heart hammering against his ribs like a trapped bird.

It had been three days since he'd first stepped through. Three days of studying harder than he'd ever studied in his life. Three days of *io sono, tu sei, lui è* until the words felt less like a list and more like a rhythm. Three days of Signora Felli giving him strange looks — first surprise, then something like approval — as his answers in class grew more confident.

He was ready.

He reached out and touched the wood. Warm, as before. The handle turned easily, as if it had been waiting.

The light spilled into his room, and he stepped through.

---

Aria was waiting.

She was sitting on a bench that hadn't been there before — a stone bench carved with swirling patterns that seemed to move when he looked at them directly. The light around her was different too: more structured, as if the realm itself had grown more defined in his absence.

"You came back," she said.

"I said I would." Misha walked toward her, and the light swirled around his legs like water. "I've been studying. I think I know the present tense now. Not perfectly, but — enough."

Aria smiled. "Show me."

Misha took a breath. "Ti vedo."

I see you.

The words felt different this time. Not just sounds, but *shapes* — shapes in the air, visible for a moment before fading. The light around him responded, brightening.

"Good." Aria stood up. "Do you know what you just did?"

"I said I see you."

"You did more than that." She moved closer. "You gave the infinitive *vedere* a subject — *io*, implied in the ending of *vedo*. You gave it an object — *ti*, you specifically. You gave it a moment — the present tense, happening now. Three things the infinitive didn't have, and suddenly it's not just a word anymore. It's a statement. A fact. Something true in the world."

She touched his arm. "That's magic, Misha. That's what I couldn't do alone. I could say *vedere* until I was breathless, but without *ti* and *vedo* and the moment of saying, it was just a seed. You made it grow."

Misha felt something unfamiliar swelling in his chest. Pride? No — something else. The feeling of being *capable*. Of being able to do something that mattered.

"What else can you do?" Aria asked.

Misha thought. The present tense was vast — he'd only scratched the surface. But he knew some things. Things he'd practiced.

"Sono Misha," he said. "I am Misha."

The light swirled around him, taking on a faint golden tint.

"Sei Aria," he tried. "You are Aria."

"Sei is wrong," Aria said gently. "Remember: *lei* is the formal 'you', but with friends, with someone your age, it's *tu*. So you should say —"

"Sei Aria," Misha corrected himself. "Tu sei Aria."

"Better." Aria smiled. "But you don't need to say *tu sei*. The *tu* is already in *sei*. You're carrying it twice."

"Oh." Misha thought. "Sei Aria. Just that."

"Just that." Aria nodded. "Italian doesn't like redundancy. Every syllable earns its place."

They walked together through the realm, and Misha practiced. He pointed at things in the light — shapes, colors, things that had no name but seemed to respond to being named — and gave them Italian words.

"La luce," he said. "The light."

"La luce calda," Aria corrected. "The warm light."

"La luce calda è bella." The warm light is beautiful.

"È molto bella." It is very beautiful.

They worked through *essere* and *avere*, the two verbs that anchored everything. Misha learned the difference between *sono stanco* — I am tired, tiredness as a quality of himself — and *ho sonno* — I have sleepiness, sleepiness as a thing he carried like a stone in his pocket.

"Essere for what you are," Aria said. "Avere for what you hold. The language knows the difference, even when you don't."

They practiced questions. *Chi sei?* Who are you. *Cosa fai?* What are you doing. *Dove vai?* Where are you going. Misha learned that questions were not the opposite of statements, but the same movement reversed — a statement sent, a question invited, both requiring the same things: a someone, a moment, a direction.

They practiced prepositions — the six small words that were handles on the world. *A* for toward. *Di* for of, for belonging to. *Da* for from, for origin. *In* for inside. *Con* with. *Su* on. *Per* for, through, in order to.

"Every sentence is a kind of pointing," Aria said. "Not just at visible things, but at relationships. At the invisible architecture of how things connect."

Misha understood. He could feel it, the way the light responded differently to *parlo di te* — I speak of you — versus *parlo con te* — I speak with you. Same verb, two different prepositions, two entirely different worlds.

---

They sat on the stone bench as the realm shifted around them, the light taking on the colors of evening — soft gold, faint rose, the blue of coming twilight.

"There's something I need to tell you," Aria said quietly.

Misha turned to look at her. Her profile was against the light, her hair falling over her shoulder. She looked older than thirteen in this moment. She looked like someone who had been waiting a very long time.

"About the spell?"

"About everything." She took a breath. "I told you I made a mistake. That I said something wrong in English, and the door opened, and I came here. That's true. But it's not the whole truth."

She turned to face him.

"The mistake wasn't a grammatical error. It was a *meaning* error. I was trying to say something to someone — someone important — and I couldn't. I didn't have the words. I didn't have the tools. The English I knew wasn't enough to hold what I needed to say."

Her voice trembled, just a little.

"I was frustrated. I was angry. I thought: if I can't say it, then I don't want to speak at all. I said something to that effect — something final. Something absolute. And the door opened, and I stepped through, thinking I was choosing silence."

She looked down at her hands.

"I didn't understand that silence is its own kind of speech. That by refusing to speak, I was still saying something. The realm heard me. It responded. And here I am."

Misha was quiet. He was thinking about his own frustration with Italian — the way he'd wanted to quit, to give up, to let the language be someone else's problem. He'd never said it out loud, but he'd thought it. Often.

"What's the spell?" he asked. "What do you need to say?"

Aria met his eyes. Her expression was strange — haunted, but also hopeful, as if she'd been carrying this for so long that the weight of it had become familiar.

"It's a farewell," she said. "To the person I couldn't speak to. To the moment I failed. To everything I didn't say, everything I didn't know how to say, everything that remained unsaid between us."

She took a breath.

"It goes like this: *Se avessi saputo allora che non potevo restare, ti avrei detto addio prima. E il regno che abbiamo visto insieme non sarebbe svanito.*"

Misha didn't understand most of it. But he understood the shape of it — the sorrow, the precision, the weight of words chosen carefully and held exactly.

"What does it mean?"

"If I had known then that I couldn't stay, I would have said goodbye to you earlier. And the kingdom that we saw together would not have vanished."

Aria's eyes were wet.

"I can't say it yet," she said. "I don't have the grammar. The conditional perfect tense. The subjunctive. The relative pronouns. All the tools I need to hold that thought, to make it real, to give it the precision it deserves. I need to learn them first."

She looked at Misha.

"With you. Together."

---

Misha returned to his room with his head spinning.

The present tense he understood — mostly. The questions, the prepositions, the difference between *essere* and *avere* — he could feel the logic of it, the way the language organized meaning into precise containers.

But what Aria had said at the end — that was beyond him.

*Se avessi saputo allora che non potevo restare, ti avrei detto addio prima.*

He wrote it down phonetically, the way it sounded. *Seh av-see sa-pee-too ahn-toe keh non po-teh-voh res-tah-reh, tee ah-vree dee-toh ah-dee-oh pree-mah.*

He didn't know what *avessi* meant. Didn't know *potevo* or *restare* or *addio*. But he could feel the shape of the sentence — the sorrow, the precision, the weight of something that had to be said exactly right.

Signora Felli had told them once: *Italian is a language of precision. Every tense exists for a reason. Every mood has its purpose.*

He was starting to understand what she meant.

---

The next day in class, Signora Felli called on him.

"Misha. Coniugare il verbo *essere*, presente."

He stood up. "Io sono. Tu sei. Lui è. Noi siamo. Voi siete. Loro sono."

"Bene." She nodded. "E *avere*?"

"Io ho. Tu hai. Lui ha. Noi abbiamo. Voi avete. Loro hanno."

"Molto bene." Signora Felli actually smiled. It was a small smile, but it was there. "E le domande, Misha? Come si fa una domanda?"

"Chi sei? Cosa fai? Dove vai?" Misha recited. "Con chi? Perché? Quando?"

"Esattamente." She moved to the next student. "Anna, coniugare —"

But Misha wasn't really listening. He was thinking about the door. About the realm. About Aria's face when she'd said the spell.

He would go back today. He would learn more. He would help her say what she needed to say.

Because somewhere between *ti vedo* and *chi sei*, between the infinitives that were seeds and the present tense that was sprouting, he'd started to care.

He wanted to hear the spell.

He wanted to help her say it.

He wanted — and this was the strange part, the part he couldn't quite explain to himself — to be there when she finally spoke the words she'd been carrying for so long.

---

That afternoon, Misha stood before the door in his room.

He didn't hesitate. He opened it and stepped through.

The light was waiting.

Aria was waiting.

She was standing this time, not sitting. And around her, the light had formed structures — walls, archways, geometric shapes that looked like buildings made of nothing but illumination.

"You came back," she said again, as if she still couldn't quite believe it.

"I told you." Misha walked toward her. "I'm not going anywhere. We're going to do this. We're going to learn all of it."

"All of it?" Aria's smile was tentative, fragile. "It's a lot, Misha. There's so much I don't know. So much we'll have to learn together."

"That's okay." Misha reached out and took her hand — an impulse, almost unconscious. Her fingers were warm, her grip firm. "We have time. Right?"

Aria's smile widened. Became real.

"Yes. We have time."

She squeezed his hand.

"Then let's begin."

# Chapter 2 — The Quest Begins

---

The door opened onto a different place.

Misha had expected the same endless light, the same undefined space where Aria waited on her stone bench. But this time, the realm had grown. The light had crystallized into structures — walls of translucent gold, archways of twisted silver, a floor beneath his feet that felt like polished glass.

And beyond the structures: a horizon.

Misha walked forward, stunned. The horizon wasn't far — maybe a kilometer away — but it was there, a definite boundary between the realm and something else. Something he couldn't see yet.

"You're here."

Aria's voice came from behind him. He turned.

She stood in one of the archways, and Misha realized with a jolt that she was *growing*. Not physically — she was still thirteen, still the same height he'd last seen. But she looked *older*. Her face had lost some of its childish softness. Her eyes held knowledge they hadn't held before.

"The realm is changing," she said, answering his unspoken question. "As you learn, it grows. As I learn, it develops. We're building it together."

She walked toward him, and the light around her shifted, forming and reforming in patterns he couldn't quite follow.

"What have you been learning?" she asked.

"The past tense." Misha had spent the past week studying *imperfetto* and *passato prossimo* until his eyes crossed. "I know the difference between *ero* and *ho stato*. Between *andavo* and *sono andato*."

"Show me." Aria stopped in front of him. "Show me the past."

Misha took a breath. He thought about how to describe it, how to make the grammar real. Then he spoke:

"Ero qui." I was here. "Brillavi." You were shining.

The realm responded. The light around them dimmed, took on a warmer tone, like memory made visible.

"Imperfetto is the camera in the past," Aria said softly. "It's the background, the ongoing state, the way things *were* rather than what *happened*. Tell me more."

Misha closed his eyes, remembering. "Mentre brillavi, sono arrivato." While you were shining, I arrived.

"Good." Aria's voice was approval, encouragement. "Now the event. Passato prossimo — the flash in the darkness. What happened next?"

"Ho visto." I saw. "Sei andata via." You went away.

The light swirled around them, responding to the verbs. Misha could feel it — the way the realm itself was learning the grammar, was taking in the distinction between background and event, between state and action.

"You're getting it." Aria moved closer. "Do you understand why there are two past tenses? Why Italian needs both?"

"Because they do different things." Misha opened his eyes. "Imperfetto is for how things *were*. Passato prossimo is for what *happened*. You need both to tell a story."

"Yes." Aria's smile was proud. "And do you know what else you need?"

Misha thought. "Trapassato?"

"Trapassato prossimo." Aria nodded. "The past of the past. The deeper layer. The thing that happened before the other past thing."

Misha took a breath. "Quando ti ho cercato, eri già andata." When I looked for you, you had already gone.

The light around them rippled, like a stone dropped in water.

"Yes." Aria's voice was fierce. "That's it. That's the depth. The moment when you realize you were too late. When you understand that the thing you wanted — the moment you wanted — is already gone."

She looked away, toward the horizon.

"That's the past, Misha. Not just what happened. But the *order* in which things happened. The *sequence*. The realization that some moments are lost before you even know to look for them."

Misha was quiet. He was thinking about his own life — about the moments he'd missed, the things he hadn't said, the opportunities lost before he knew they were opportunities.

"The future too," he said suddenly. "I learned the future too. *Sarò. Andrò. Vedrò.*"

Aria turned back to him. "Show me."

"Sarò qui." I will be here. "Ti vedrò." I will see you. "Restirò con te." I will stay with you.

As he spoke, the realm changed. The horizon shimmered, became less distinct, as if the future were visible but not yet clear. The light around them took on a quality of *becoming* — not quite one thing, not quite another, always in motion.

"The future is a promise," Aria said. "But it's also a threat. Because the future might not happen. The future *might have been* different."

She took a breath.

"Condizionale. The conditional tense. Do you know it?"

"Vorrei." I would like. "Potrei." I could. "Dovrei." I should.

"Those are the simple forms." Aria's voice dropped. "But there's another one. The conditional perfect. *Avevo detto che saresti tornato.* I had said that you would return. *Sarei rimasto.* I would have stayed."

Misha felt a chill. "What's the difference?"

"Condizionale semplice is a wish about the present. *Vorrei restare* — I would like to stay, right now, in this moment." Aria moved closer, her eyes locked on his. "But condizionale composto is a wish about the past. Something you wanted but didn't do. Something that *can't happen anymore*. *Sarei rimasto con te* — I would have stayed with you. But I didn't. And now I can't."

Misha understood. The weight of it. The sorrow.

"The future in the past," he said slowly. "That's what Signora Felli called it. When you're talking about something that was in the future from the perspective of the past, but it never happened."

"Yes." Aria's eyes were bright with unshed tears. "*Avevi detto che saresti tornato.* You had said that you would return. But you didn't. The future you promised — the future I believed in — it didn't happen. And I need to be able to say that. I need to be able to name the disappointment. The broken promise. The future that died."

She reached out and took his hands.

"This is why I need you, Misha. This is why I can't do it alone. I need to learn the conditional perfect. I need to learn how to say what *would have been*. And I can't learn it alone — the realm requires two minds, two voices, to teach the grammar of regret."

Misha squeezed her hands. "Then we'll learn it together."

---

They walked toward the horizon.

As they approached, Misha saw that it wasn't a boundary at all. It was a doorway — or would be, when the time was right. The realm wasn't ending here; it was *continuing*. The horizon was just the edge of what they could currently see, the limit of their current understanding.

"What's beyond?" he asked.

"The rest." Aria didn't elaborate. She didn't need to.

They sat on a bench that formed beneath them — the realm responding to their need, providing what was required before they could even ask for it.

"I should tell you more," Aria said quietly. "About who I was. Before."

"You were Italian."

"I was. From Florence." A small smile. "My parents were academics. They taught Italian literature, language, culture. I grew up with Dante and Boccaccio as bedtime stories. Italian wasn't just a language — it was inheritance. It was birthright."

She looked down at her hands.

"I was good at it. Better than good. I spoke perfectly, read fluently, wrote with a voice that was already forming. My parents were proud. They said I would follow in their footsteps. That I would teach Italian, write about Italian, *be* Italian in a way that mattered."

She took a breath.

"Then we moved. To London, for my father's research. And suddenly I was the Italian girl — the exotic one, the one with the accent, the one who didn't quite fit. My English was good, but not good enough. Not *fluent* enough. Not *native* enough."

She looked at Misha.

"There was a boy. His name was Thomas. We were — friends. Or I thought we were. I wanted to tell him something. Something important. But I didn't have the words. My English wasn't enough to hold the feeling. And his Italian was nonexistent."

She swallowed.

"I tried anyway. I used the words I had. They were the wrong words. They didn't mean what I wanted them to mean. Thomas didn't understand — or maybe he understood exactly the wrong thing. He laughed. Not cruelly, but — carelessly. Like it didn't matter. Like what I was trying to say didn't matter."

She was crying now, silent tears tracking down her cheeks.

"I was so angry. So *humiliated*. I thought: if I can't say it, then I don't want to say anything. I said something to that effect — something final. Something absolute. And the door opened, and I stepped through."

She looked at Misha.

"I've been here ever since. Learning. Growing. Waiting for someone to help me say what I couldn't say then. To say it right this time. To say it *well*."

Misha didn't know what to say. He wanted to comfort her, but the words felt inadequate. So he did the only thing he could think of: he spoke in Italian.

"Ti sento." I feel you. "Ti capisco." I understand you. "Sono qui con te." I am here with you.

The realm responded. The light around them swirled, formed shapes — trees, maybe, or columns of light. Structures of support.

Aria wiped her eyes. "Thank you."

"Is Thomas who you need to say goodbye to?" Misha asked quietly.

Aria shook her head. "No. Thomas was — the catalyst. The trigger. But the goodbye isn't for him. It's for the version of me that couldn't speak. That couldn't find the words. That felt so small, so inadequate, so *less than*."

She looked at him.

"The spell is for the girl who stepped through the door. The girl who thought silence was better than speaking badly. The girl who didn't understand that speaking badly is better than not speaking at all."

She squeezed his hands.

"I need to tell her: *Se avessi saputo allora che non potevo restare, ti avrei detto addio prima. E il regno che abbiamo visto insieme non sarebbe svanito.*"

"If I had known then that I couldn't stay, I would have said goodbye to you earlier. And the kingdom that we saw together would not have vanished."

Misha felt the weight of it. The sorrow, the precision, the unbearable rightness of the words.

"We'll learn it," he said. "I promise. We'll learn all of it. Imperfetto and passato prossimo and trapassato and futuro semplice and futuro anteriore and condizionale semplice and condizionale composto. We'll learn every tense, every mood, every tool Italian has for building meaning."

He squeezed her hands back.

"And then you'll say it. You'll say the spell. And you'll be free."

---

Misha returned to Moscow with his head full of tenses.

He understood now why Italian had so many past tenses. Why it needed both *imperfetto* and *passato prossimo*. Why *trapassato* existed as a deeper layer of past. Why the future wasn't just *futuro semplice* but also *futuro anteriore* — the future perfect, the completed future.

He understood why *condizionale composto* mattered. The conditional perfect. The tense of regret. The tense of *would have*. The tense for naming the things that didn't happen but could have, the paths not taken, the futures that died before they were born.

Signora Felli had never explained it that way. She'd taught the mechanics, the conjugations, the rules. But she'd never explained *why* it mattered. Why you would need a tense for broken promises, for unrealized wishes, for the architecture of regret.

Now he knew.

Now he had a reason to learn.

---

That night, Misha dreamed.

He was in the realm, but it was different — larger, more complex. The light had formed buildings, streets, entire cities made of nothing but illumination. And Aria was there, walking beside him, older now, almost grown.

They were speaking Italian, but it was Italian beyond anything he'd learned — sentences of incredible complexity, weaving together tenses and moods, building thoughts of crystalline precision.

*Quando ero piccola,* she was saying, *pensavo che il linguaggio fosse una cosa semplice. Credetti che se avessi saputo abbastanza parole, avrei potuto dire tutto.*

When I was little, I thought language was a simple thing. I believed that if I knew enough words, I could say everything.

*Ma non è vero,* Misha heard himself reply. *Le parole non bastano. Ci vogliono gli strumenti. La grammatica. La precisione.*

But it's not true. Words are not enough. You need tools. Grammar. Precision.

*Esatto,* Aria said. *E quando hai tutti gli strumenti, quando puoi dire esattamente quello che vuoi dire, niente di meno e niente di più — allora sì. Allora puoi parlare la verità.*

Exactly. And when you have all the tools, when you can say exactly what you want to say, nothing less and nothing more — then yes. Then you can speak the truth.

Misha woke with tears on his face.

He didn't remember all of the dream. But he remembered the feeling — the sense that Italian was not just a language but a *way* of thinking, a method of building meaning with the precision of architecture, the care of poetry.

He lay in bed, watching the Moscow dawn filter through his curtains, and thought about Aria. Thought about the spell. Thought about the girl who had stepped through a door because she couldn't find the right words.

He would help her.

He would learn everything.

He would give her the tools to say what she needed to say.

# Chapter 1 — Now

---

The first thing language needed was a center.

Not a rule. Not a system. A *center* — a fixed point from which everything else could be measured. A place called *here* and a moment called *now* and, most importantly, a someone called *I* who stood inside both.

Without this, you have infinitives. Floating seeds. Stones without hands.

With it, you have a sentence. And a sentence, even a small one, is already the beginning of a world.

---

It started with a look.

Iro looked at Ua across the dimming space between them. Not the vague awareness they'd had before — two presences noting each other in passing. A *look*. Directed. Intentional. The kind of looking that says: I have turned my attention toward you specifically, and I am doing it now, in this moment, and not in any other.

Something moved in Iro. The infinitive *{it}vedere{/it}* — to see — was changing shape. It was no longer a free-floating concept. It was acquiring a person and a moment. It was becoming, without Iro quite understanding how, a verb in the present tense.

*{it}Ti vedo{/it}.*

I see you.

The words crossed the space between them and landed.

Ua went very still.

Because *{it}ti vedo{/it}* is not like *{it}vedere{/it}*. *{it}Vedere{/it}* belongs to everyone and no one. *{it}Ti vedo{/it}* belongs to this exact moment, this exact person doing the seeing, and this exact person being seen. Three small words containing: a subject (implied in the verb's ending — *{it}vedo{/it}* already means *I see*, the pronoun is folded inside), a direct object (*{it}ti{/it}* — you, specifically you, the person I am looking at), and a tense (present, happening now, not before, not after, *now*).

*{it}Ti vedo{/it}* is a declaration of presence. I am here. You are here. This is happening between us.

For the first time in the fading world, something had been said that could only be said by one specific being to one other specific being at one specific moment. The infinitive was universal. The present tense verb was *theirs*.

---

Ua wanted to respond. Felt the shape of a response forming — not words yet, but the pressure behind words, the urgency of having something to give back.

What came out first was not a verb. It was a question.

*{it}Chi sei?{/it}*

Who are you.

Three words again. But this time the three words did something different — they reached. *{it}Vedere{/it}* lands. *{it}Chi sei{/it}* reaches outward, makes a space in the air the shape of an answer, waits to be filled. Iro had given Ua a fact. Ua was giving Iro a container.

Questions, Iro would slowly understand, were not the opposite of statements. They were the same movement, reversed. A statement sends. A question invites. Both require the same thing: a someone, a moment, a direction.

*{it}Sono Iro{/it}*, Iro said.

I am Iro.

And in the saying — this is the strange thing about *{it}essere{/it}*, about the verb to be — Iro became slightly more so. Because to say *{it}sono{/it}* is not merely to report. It is to place yourself in the present tense, to claim your own existence as a current, ongoing fact. *{it}Sono{/it}* does not mean *I existed* or *I will exist*. It means: *right now, in this moment we are sharing, I am.*

Ua understood this immediately, the way you understand gravity — not by thinking about it, but by noticing you are standing.

*{it}Sono Ua{/it}*, Ua said.

They had names now. And the present tense. And each other.

---

But the world was still fading.

And as the light withdrew from one edge, then another, Iro and Ua began to notice something that pure verbs could not capture: *difference*. Not everything was the same. Not all light was alike. Not all darkness threatened equally.

They needed to describe.

Ua looked at the light that remained — a particular quality of it, warm and close, different from the cold distant shine that had already left — and reached for something alongside the noun.

*{it}Luce calda{/it}*, Ua said. *{it}La luce calda{/it}.*

Warm light. The warm light.

Two things happened in that small addition that would take years to fully understand.

First: the adjective. *{it}Calda{/it}* — warm — did not just add information. It committed. It said: this light is not light in general, it is *this kind* of light, and I am willing to name its quality and be held to the naming. Every adjective is a small act of precision. You look at something, you decide what it essentially is, and you say so.

Second: the article. *{it}La{/it}* — the. Not *{it}una luce calda{/it}*, some warm light, a warm light belonging to no particular story. *{it}La luce calda{/it}* — the warm light, the specific one, the one we are both looking at, the one we share. The definite article is a tiny miracle. It says: this noun has already entered our conversation, it lives in the space between us now, it belongs to our shared attention.

*{it}Una stella{/it}*, Ua said, pointing.

*{it}La stella{/it}*, Iro answered.

A star — unknown, newly noticed. *The* star — the same star, now known, now shared, now *theirs*.

The article, Iro slowly understood, was not a grammatical formality. It was memory made visible. Every time you say *the*, you are saying: we both know what I'm talking about. We have been here together long enough to have things in common. The definite article is the grammatical proof that you are not alone.

---

They practiced. The world around them was full of things that needed naming and qualities that needed attaching.

*{it}La luce è bella{/it}*, Ua said.

The light is beautiful.

*{it}Essere{/it}* again — this time not as *I am* but as *the light is*. The verb that joins a subject to its quality. Iro understood: there are two ways to speak about states of being. You can say *{it}sono stanco{/it}* — I am tired, tiredness as a quality of my self, something I essentially am right now. Or you can say *{it}ho sonno{/it}* — I have sleepiness, sleepiness as a thing I carry, an object of possession rather than a property of identity.

*{it}Essere{/it}* for what you are. *{it}Avere{/it}* for what you hold.

This distinction would take a long time to feel natural. It cut across the grain of intuition, which wanted to say *I am cold* and instead was told to say *I have cold*, to experience coldness as a possession rather than a condition. But slowly the logic revealed itself: some states belong to the self, are *of* the self. Others arrive from outside, are carried, are had. The language was not wrong. The language was, in fact, more precise than the intuition.

---

The world grew smaller as the light withdrew. And as it grew smaller, distance — actual physical distance — became something that mattered.

Iro and Ua were not standing in the same place. They had, for reasons neither could fully explain, remained apart. There was space between them. And the space was not nothing. The space had qualities and directions and relationships that needed names.

*{it}Vengo da te{/it}*, Ua said one day, moving closer.

I am coming from you — toward you — coming *from my place* to *yours*.

The preposition arrived like a gift. *{it}Da{/it}* — from, since, by, via. A tiny word carrying enormous cargo: origin, direction, duration, agency. And when it found the article — *{it}dal{/it}*, *{it}dalla{/it}*, *{it}dall'{/it}* — it fused, became one word, the two ideas inseparable.

*{it}A{/it}* — to, at, toward. *{it}Di{/it}* — of, about, belonging to. *{it}In{/it}* — in, inside, within. *{it}Con{/it}* — with, together, by means of. *{it}Su{/it}* — on, above, upon. *{it}Per{/it}* — for, through, in order to.

Six small words. Six directions the mind can move in relation to any object. Six handles on the world.

Iro began to understand these not as vocabulary but as something older — the directions hands can point. *{it}A{/it}* points toward. *{it}Di{/it}* points inward, to the inside of a thing. *{it}Da{/it}* points back, to where something came from. *{it}Con{/it}* points sideways, to what accompanies. *{it}Su{/it}* points up. *{it}Per{/it}* points forward, toward purpose.

Every sentence, Iro realized, was a kind of pointing. Not at visible things only, but at relationships. At the invisible architecture of how things connected.

*{it}Parlo di te{/it}*, Iro said.

I speak of you. The *{it}di{/it}* carrying the thought inward, toward the inside of the subject, toward what the words were *about*.

*{it}Parlo con te{/it}*, Ua answered.

I speak with you. The *{it}con{/it}* placing them beside each other, in the same motion, together.

Same verb. Two different prepositions. Two entirely different worlds.

---

They learned *{it}voglio{/it}* and *{it}posso{/it}* and *{it}devo{/it}* — I want, I can, I must. The modal verbs, which are not actions but *relationships to actions*. Not what you do but what you wish to do, are able to do, are required to do.

*{it}Voglio restare{/it}*, Iro said.

I want to stay.

It was the first time *{it}restare{/it}* — Ua's word, the word for staying — had been claimed by a person and a desire. No longer floating. Now belonging to a wish. To a will.

But *{it}voglio restare{/it}* was only a statement of desire. It didn't say: *I will stay*. Didn't say: *I am staying*. Didn't guarantee anything about what would actually happen. The modal keeps its subject at a slight distance from the action, a distance that is honest — because wanting something and doing it are not the same thing, and the language knew this before they did.

Then Ua said something new.

*{it}Vorrei dirti qualcosa{/it}.*

I would like to tell you something.

*{it}Vorrei{/it}.* Not *{it}voglio{/it}* — the clean present tense of desire. *{it}Vorrei{/it}* — the desire held slightly further away, made softer, made more tentative. The conditional of *{it}volere{/it}*. I would want. I would like. A wish in the space of the possible rather than the space of the actual.

Iro felt something shift at *{it}vorrei{/it}*. Felt a door opening that wasn't open before. Because *{it}vorrei{/it}* was the first word that had reached, grammatically, toward the hypothetical. It was not a hypothesis yet. But it was the gesture. The hand extended in the direction of: what if.

What if things were other than they are.

What if I could hold this moment.

What if the light came back.

Iro wasn't ready to say any of this. The words didn't exist yet. But *vorrei* was the first word that aimed in that direction, and the aiming was something.

---

*{it}Resta{/it}*, Iro said one evening.

Stay. Imperative. Direct. The command form of *{it}restare{/it}*, the verb Ua had always known.

It was not, technically, a hypothesis. It was a request. An instruction directed at a specific person in the specific present. *Stay*. Do this thing. Now. The imperative is the most direct form of language — there is no subject, because the subject is obvious (it is *you*, always *you*), and there is no tense, because the time is obvious (it is *now*, always *now*).

*{it}Resta{/it}* was the most that Iro could say.

It was not enough. Iro knew it was not enough. Because *resta* presupposed that Ua was choosing to leave — that the disappearance was a decision, a movement Ua was making, something that a command could stop. And Iro was beginning to understand that it wasn't that. It was something else. Something in the world itself, in the light itself, in the nature of things that fade.

You cannot command the light to stay with an imperative.

You need something more complex than *resta*.

You need, specifically, a sentence about what you would have done differently if you had known what was coming — a sentence that exists not in the present tense but in the deepest conditional, the one reserved for things that can no longer be changed.

Iro didn't have that sentence yet.

But Iro could feel its shape. Could feel the exact place in the mind where the language wasn't yet, the gap in the architecture, the room with no walls.

*{it}Ti vedo{/it}*, Iro said instead. *{it}Sei qui. Voglio restare con te{/it}.*

I see you. You are here. I want to stay with you.

It was the most complete truth available with the tools at hand.

The light continued, quietly, to withdraw.

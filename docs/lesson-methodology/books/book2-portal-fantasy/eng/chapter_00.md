# Chapter 0 — The Door

---

Misha hated {it}Italian{/it}.

He hated the way the words stuck in his throat like fish bones. He hated the {it}conjugations{/it} that sprawled across his textbook pages like maps to a country he'd never visit. He hated that his teacher, {it}Signora Felli{/it}, made them stand up and repeat {it}*io sono, tu sei, lui è*{/it} like it was a prayer instead of grammar.

Most of all, he hated that his mother cared.

"You have a gift for languages," she would say, in that careful voice she used when she wanted to believe something she knew wasn't true. "{it}Russian{/it}, {it}English{/it} — you learned those so easily. {it}Italian{/it} will come."

Misha, age twelve and rapidly developing an expertise in the many forms of parental delusion, would nod and say nothing. What he wanted to say was: *I don't want {it}Italian{/it} to come. I want it to go away and leave me alone with my books and my games and the {it}Moscow{/it} winter that made sense.*

They lived on the seventh floor of a concrete block on {it}Novy Arbat{/it}. Below their window, the city churned — cars, snow, people hurrying to places that mattered more than {it}conjugations{/it}. Misha spent his afternoons at his desk, the textbook open like a trap, {it}Signora Felli{/it}'s voice echoing in his head: *Misha, attention. The {it}verb{/it} does not lie to you. You must listen.*

He didn't want to listen. He wanted to disappear.

---

The door appeared on a Tuesday.

Misha was supposed to be studying. *{it}Essere{/it}* — to be. *{it}Avere{/it}* — to have. The two {it}verbs{/it} that held the {it}Italian{/it} language together, the ones you had to memorize before you could say anything at all. He'd been staring at the {it}conjugation{/it} chart for twenty minutes, and none of it was sticking.

*{it}Io sono. Tu sei. Lui è.}{/it**

The letters were starting to swim. Misha rubbed his eyes. The radiator beneath the window hissed and clanked — the building's heating system, old as the {it}Soviet{/it} Union itself, complaining about the cold outside.

He looked up.

There was a door in the corner of his room.

Not against the wall. *In* the corner, where two walls met at an angle that shouldn't have accommodated anything but air. The door was wood, dark and weathered, with iron hinges and a handle that looked like it had been touched by a thousand hands before his.

Misha stood up. His heart was doing something strange in his chest.

*This isn't possible,* he thought. *There is no door in my room. There has never been a door.*

But there it was. And the longer he looked, the more it seemed not new at all — as if it had always been there, waiting for him to notice. Waiting for something.

He reached out. His fingers brushed the wood. It was warm.

The handle turned. The door swung open.

---

What he saw on the other side was not a room.

It was light.

Not the warm yellow light of his desk lamp. Not the harsh white light of the {it}Moscow{/it} streetlamps. Not the gray light of a winter afternoon. This light was *alive* — it moved and shifted like water, like something that was aware of being seen. It filled the space beyond the door completely, and it seemed to go on forever.

Misha stepped through.

---

The light had a smell.

That was the first thing he noticed, after the shock of being somewhere that couldn't exist. It smelled like lemons and old books and rain on pavement. It smelled like something he remembered from a dream he'd had as a small child, before his father left, before {it}Moscow{/it} became just one city instead of *the* city.

He took another step. The door had closed behind him — he could hear the click, soft but final — and now he was standing in the light, and the light was standing in him.

"Misha," said a voice.

He turned. There was a girl standing a few meters away. She was about his age, maybe a year older, with dark hair that fell past her shoulders and eyes the color of honey. She wore clothes he couldn't place — a tunic of soft blue fabric, leather sandals laced up her calves. She looked like she'd stepped out of a painting, or a story, or a dream.

"Who are you?" he asked.

The girl tilted her head. "My name is {it}Aria{/it}. I've been waiting for you."

"Waiting?" Misha looked around wildly. The light extended in all directions, no walls, no ceiling, no floor that he could see. Just light, and {it}Aria{/it}, and the sense that this place was enormous. "Where is this? How did I get here?"

"You walked through the door." {it}Aria{/it} smiled, and it was the kind of smile that made him feel like she knew things he didn't, but wouldn't make him feel stupid for not knowing them. "The door appears when someone is needed. When someone is *ready*."

"Ready for what?"

"To learn." She took a step closer. "To speak."

Misha laughed bitterly. "I'm the last person who should be here. I can't learn {it}Italian{/it}. I've tried. It doesn't work."

"That's because you're trying to memorize." {it}Aria{/it} reached out and touched his arm, and he felt a spark like static electricity but warmer. "{it}Italian{/it} isn't something you memorize. It's something you *do*. It's something you *become*."

She stepped back. "Show me. Say something."

"Like what?"

"Anything. A word. A sound. Whatever is in you."

Misha thought. The only {it}Italian{/it} words he could reliably remember were the {it}infinitives{/it} — the dictionary forms, the {it}verbs{/it} in their state of pure potential, before they'd been claimed by a person or a moment.

"{it}Andare,{/it}" he said. "To go."

The moment the word left his lips, something changed.

The light shifted. Swirled. Responded.

*{it}Andare{/it}.* The word hung in the air like a visible thing, like a thread of gold. Misha stared. He'd said it a hundred times in class, repeating after {it}Signora Felli{/it}, and it had never felt like *this*.

"It listens," {it}Aria{/it} said softly. "The realm listens. Do you understand? Every word is a spell. Every sentence is a door. But only if you say it *right*."

"Right?"

"Accurately. Truly. {it}Italian{/it} is not a language of approximation. It is a language of precision. When you say *{it}io vado{/it}* — I go — you are not just describing an action. You are committing to it. You are placing yourself in motion, in this moment, with all the weight of a person who goes."

Misha felt dizzy. "I don't know how to do that. I barely know the {it}present tense{/it}."

"Then we start with what you know." {it}Aria{/it}'s expression changed — became serious, intent. "The {it}infinitives{/it} are the seeds. They are pure potential, waiting to grow. Show me what seeds you have."

Misha took a breath. "{it}Essere,{/it}" he said. "To be."

The light shimmered.

"{it}Avere,{/it}" he said. "To have."

The light swirled around him, responding to each word.

"{it}Fare. Vedere. Sapere.{/it}"

*To do. To see. To know.*

With each word, the realm reacted. Misha could feel it — could feel the light drinking in the sounds, responding to the shapes his mouth made. It was the strangest thing he'd ever felt, and also the most natural. Like the light had been waiting a long time to hear these words spoken correctly.

"Good," {it}Aria{/it} said. "Very good. The seeds take root."

"But they're just {it}infinitives{/it}," Misha protested. "They don't mean anything. They're not sentences. They're not *communication*."

"Everything begins with the {it}infinitive{/it}." {it}Aria{/it} moved closer again, and this time she took both his hands in hers. Her palms were warm, her grip firm. "Misha, do you know why you're here? Why the door opened for you?"

He shook his head.

"Because there is something I need to say." Her voice trembled, just a little. "Something I need to tell someone. And I can't say it alone. I need help. I need..." She searched for the word. "I need a partner."

"A partner for what?"

"For the spell." {it}Aria{/it} looked into his eyes, and he saw that hers were wet with tears she wouldn't let fall. "For the great spell. The complete spell. The one that requires all of {it}Italian{/it} — all the {it}tenses{/it}, all the {it}moods{/it}, all the precision of this language — to say what must be said."

Misha's mouth was dry. "What spell?"

{it}Aria{/it} swallowed. "The spell of farewell."

She released his hands and stepped back. "I am trapped here, Misha. In this realm. I have been here a long time — longer than I can count. The only way out is to speak the spell correctly. To say what I need to say, in {it}Italian{/it}, with absolute precision. But I can't do it alone. The spell requires two voices. Two minds. Two people who have learned the language together."

She took a deep breath. "Will you help me?"

Misha looked at her — really looked. The girl who appeared from nowhere, in a realm made of light, asking for help with a spell he didn't understand. He should have been afraid. He should have turned around, found the door, gone back to his room and his textbook and his ordinary life.

But he didn't want to.

"I don't know if I can," he said honestly. "I'm not good at this. I'm not good at languages."

{it}Aria{/it} smiled. "You're already better than you think. The light responds to you. It *likes* you. That matters more than you know."

She extended her hand. "Come. Let me show you the realm. Let me show you what happens when you speak {it}Italian{/it} *{it}bene{/it}* — when you speak it well, when you speak it truly."

Misha looked at her hand. Small, calloused, capable.

He took it.

---

The realm was not just light.

As they walked, Misha began to see that the light took different forms in different places. Here it swirled like water, translucent blue. There it crystallized into golden geometric shapes that hung in the air like three-dimensional thoughts. Everywhere, it responded to {it}Aria{/it}'s presence — brightening when she smiled, dimming when she was serious, shifting color when she spoke.

"What is this place?" he asked.

"This is the Realm of {it}Italian{/it}." {it}Aria{/it} kept walking, her sandals making no sound on ground that wasn't quite ground. "Every language has a realm. Every language is a doorway to somewhere else. Most people never see them. They just use the words — badly, half-heartedly, without precision. But the realms are always there. Waiting."

"How did you find it?"

{it}Aria{/it} was silent for a moment. When she spoke, her voice was quieter. "I didn't find it. It found me. I was — am — {it}Italian{/it}. From {it}Italy{/it}, the real {it}Italy{/it}. And I was studying {it}English{/it}, trying to learn it, and one day I..." She stopped. "I made a mistake. I said something wrong. And the door opened, and I stepped through, and I've been here ever since."

"You're trapped?"

"I'm waiting." She looked at him. "I'm waiting for someone to learn with me. To grow with me. Because the spell I need to say — the farewell — it's not simple {it}Italian{/it}. It's not something you learn in a week. It requires everything. Every {it>tense{/it}. Every {it}mood{/it}. Every way {it}Italian{/it} has of expressing meaning."

She stopped walking and turned to face him. "Misha, do you know why {it}Italian grammar{/it} is so complicated? Why there are so many {it>tenses{/it}, so many {it>irregular verbs{/it}, so many ways to say the same thing differently?"

He shook his head.

"Because meaning is complicated." {it}Aria{/it}'s voice took on the rhythm of someone who had thought about this for a very long time. "There is a difference between *I am* and *I was*. There is a difference between *I would* and *I would have*. There is a difference between *I hope that you are* and *I hope that you might be*. {it}Italian{/it} doesn't just give you words. It gives you *tools* — precise tools, for building exact meanings. The more you learn, the more you can say. The more you can say, the more you can *feel*."

She reached out and touched his cheek. Her fingers were gentle.

"I have a thought in my mind," she said softly. "A thought I've been carrying for a very long time. It's complete. It's whole. It's everything I want to say. But I can't say it yet. I don't have the tools. I need to learn them first. And I need to learn them with someone else."

She stepped back, her hand falling to her side.

"Will you come back?" she asked. "Will you return through the door, and keep learning {it}Italian{/it}, and help me build the spell?"

Misha looked at her. Looked at the light that swirled around them, responding to her presence. Thought of his textbook, open on his desk in {it}Moscow{/it}. Thought of {it}Signora Felli{/it}'s voice, saying *The {it>verb{/it} does not lie to you.*

He realized something: he had never hated {it}Italian{/it}. He had hated *failing* at {it>Italian{/it}. He had hated feeling stupid, feeling slow, feeling like everyone else understood something he couldn't grasp.

But here, with {it>Aria{/it}, in this place where words were visible and {it>grammar{/it} was magic...

"I'll come back," he said. "I promise."

{it}Aria{/it}'s smile was like the sun coming out.

"Good," she said. "Then there's something you should know."

"What?"

"The door works both ways." She pointed, and Misha saw it — another door, identical to the one in his room, standing in the middle of the light. "When you're ready to return, just open it. You'll be back in {it>Moscow{/it}. And when you want to come here, you only need to speak {it>Italian{/it} — *true* {it>Italian{/it}, {it>Italian{/it} you mean — and the door will appear."

She took his hands again. "Misha, listen to me. This matters. What we're doing here — it matters. The spell is important. But more important than the spell is the learning. The growing. The becoming."

"How long will it take?"

{it}Aria{/it}'s smile faded. "I don't know. As long as it takes. The spell can't be rushed. Every piece has to be in place. Every tool has to be mastered."

"Will I be able to say it too? The spell?"

"If you learn with me, yes." She squeezed his hands. "It belongs to both of us. It has to — that's part of the magic."

Misha nodded. He didn't fully understand, but he would learn.

"I should go," he said. "My mother — she'll wonder where I am."

"Go." {it}Aria{/it} released him. "But remember: the door opens for those who speak. Come back when you've learned something new. When you've grown."

Misha walked to the door. His hand was on the handle when {it>Aria{/it} called out:

"Misha?"

He turned.

"The {it>infinitives{/it} are good," she said. "But they're only the beginning. To truly speak {it>Italian{/it} — to do magic — you need more than seeds. You need to learn how to make them grow."

She smiled.

"I'll see you soon."

---

Misha opened the door.

His room was exactly as he'd left it. The textbook was open on his desk. The radiator hissed. The window showed the dark {it>Moscow{/it} street, the snow falling silently.

He sat down at his desk and looked at the {it>conjugation{/it} chart.

*{it>Io sono. Tu sei. Lui è.}{/it**

For the first time, he saw it differently. Not as a list to memorize. Not as a hurdle to clear. As a tool. As something that could *do* something.

The {it>infinitives{/it} were seeds.

The {it>present tense{/it} was the first sprout.

And beyond that — {it>tenses{/it} he'd only heard of. {it>Past tense{/it}. {it>Future tense{/it}. {it>Conditional tense{/it}. {it>Subjunctive mood{/it}. Tools he couldn't imagine, waiting to be learned.

Misha picked up his pen. He wrote something in the margin of his textbook, in {it>Italian{/it}:

*{it>Essere. Avere. Andare. Venire. Fare. Vedere. Sapere. Potere. Volere.{/it}*

The seeds.

Then he wrote something else, in {it>Russian{/it}, in smaller letters:

*The door opens for those who speak.*

He would learn.

He would grow.

He would return.
